When Seven Properties Become Seven Problems

A property portfolio of seven buy-to-lets — all repossessed. Then came the shortfalls, the charging orders, the HMRC claim, the endless letters and phone calls. Mr. & Mrs. TA needed someone to help them see through it.

The Situation

How it started

The letters arrived every day. I’d see the envelopes on the mat and pile them on my desk, telling myself I’d deal with them later. Later became weeks, and weeks became months, until there was a pile on my desk I simply could not face.

Then the calls started — on my mobile, on the landline. They seemed to know when I was home. I’d put the phone down and it would ring again within minutes. I stopped answering altogether.

I had built up a portfolio of seven buy-to-let properties over many years. It felt like security — something solid, something that was ours. But one by one, things unravelled. All seven were repossessed. And instead of that drawing a line under it, the shortfalls followed us home. HMRC had a claim against a family trust we’d held for years. Two of the mortgage lenders had already been to court and obtained final charging orders against our family home. There were letters in French from solicitors acting on two overseas properties. The debt wasn’t just large — it was everywhere, and it kept multiplying.

Looking back, I know I was suffering from mild depression. At the time, I just thought I was struggling to cope. Every new letter, every missed call felt like proof that I had failed. I couldn’t see a way through. My wife was frightened too, and I had nothing to tell her. I was caught in a loop — the more I ignored it, the worse it became, and the worse it became, the harder it was to look at. A friend of my wife’s mentioned Lightside. I was reluctant to call. But eventually, I did.

What We Did and Why It Worked

The work behind the outcome

When we first met Mr. TA, he was wary. Depression and physical pain had made it hard for him to trust that anyone could genuinely help. We started somewhere practical: we sat with him and worked through the letters. Not a summary — the letters themselves, physically, every envelope. We sorted through the pile and shredded what was no longer relevant. Two black bin bags’ worth. What remained was a manageable stack of live issues that could actually be addressed. Mr. TA told us afterwards that he felt lighter almost immediately. Mrs. TA said the same.

The structural problem was the family home. Two BTL mortgage lenders had obtained final charging orders, and bailiff action had been threatened. The equity was being steadily eroded. In most cases, we look for ways to retain the family home — but here the calculus was different. A forced sale by creditors would have left far less for the family and none of the control. We advised a controlled sale instead, with proceeds distributed to the adult children in line with the terms of a trust that was already in place. It was an unusual recommendation, but it preserved the family’s position.

The HMRC claim against the family trust required specialist input. We brought in Tax Gains, a tax investigations firm, whose work reduced the HMRC liability to zero. The remaining unsecured debts were placed under a creditor arrangement — repayments agreed and reviewed annually. There has been no further enforcement action, and no bankruptcy petition was ever issued. Mr. TA is working again, his health has improved considerably, and he enjoys spending time with his grandchildren.

Creditor arrangements — what people most want to know

A creditor arrangement is an informal agreement reached between a debtor and their creditors — either directly or through a debt adviser — setting out how and when debts will be repaid. It is not a formal insolvency procedure, which means it does not appear on a public register in the same way as an IVA or bankruptcy. The terms vary: repayments may be fixed monthly amounts, annual lump sums, or a combination, depending on what the creditors agree and what the debtor can genuinely sustain. A good creditor arrangement resolves the debt without the lasting consequences of formal insolvency — but it requires creditors to agree, which is where an experienced adviser matters.

When a buy-to-let property is repossessed and sold, the mortgage lender will recover what they can from the sale. If the sale proceeds do not cover the full outstanding mortgage balance, the remaining shortfall becomes an unsecured debt — and the lender can pursue it through the courts just like any other unsecured creditor. If several properties are repossessed, several shortfalls can accumulate quickly, alongside any other unsecured debts already in place. The result is often a large and complex unsecured debt position that requires a coordinated response across multiple creditors.

Yes, though it takes more work than dealing with a single creditor. Each creditor has to be contacted, assessed, and in most cases approached separately. Some will be more willing to agree than others, and a creditor who holds security such as a charging order on a property is in a different legal position to one who does not. Having all the debts mapped accurately before any approach is made is essential — it is much harder to negotiate from a position of incomplete information. An experienced adviser can handle those conversations on your behalf and ensure that the terms agreed with one creditor do not inadvertently create problems with another.

A creditor arrangement is generally the right route when the debts are manageable relative to income and assets — that is, when the underlying financial position is recoverable, even if the current situation is not. It is less likely to be appropriate where the debt is simply too large to repay from any realistic income or asset base, or where formal insolvency protections are needed urgently. The key question is not just whether you can afford to pay, but whether the creditors will agree to terms that work — which is why an honest assessment at the outset matters more than any optimistic projection.

The earlier the better, because more options remain open — but even cases where court action has already started, or where charging orders are already in place, can be resolved. An Individual Voluntary Arrangement (IVA) is a formal insolvency procedure, governed by the Insolvency Act 1986 and administered by a licensed Insolvency Practitioner. It appears on the Individual Insolvency Register and has significant consequences for credit rating and in some cases professional licences. A creditor arrangement is an informal agreement — more flexible, not publicly registered, and without the statutory framework of an IVA, although it does still impact your credit file. For some situations the formal protections of an IVA are necessary; for others, an informal arrangement achieves a practical outcome with fewer lasting consequences.
