The Situation

How it started

We got the house as a young couple with a small inheritance from my nan as the deposit. But then we started a family — a pregnancy, a toddler, and my husband’s work was unpredictable. Month to month we just didn’t have enough, so we started using credit cards to fill the gaps. We never thought of it as a problem until the bailiff turned up at the door.

We had no choice — we called Lightside. Priti worked out a Creditor Arrangement to bring all the debts together and arranged for creditors to stop the enforcement action. For a while it worked. But then our mortgage rate rose and suddenly the numbers didn’t work anymore. We were paying the arrangement, paying the mortgage, and we just couldn’t keep both going.

We knew it was bad, but we were frightened of what would happen if we couldn’t keep paying. We didn’t understand bankruptcy. We thought it would lose us our home immediately. We thought it would ruin us.

What We Did and Why It Worked

The work behind the outcome

When Mr. and Mrs. V came to us, they were trapped in a Creditor Arrangement that had worked when rates were lower, but now made no sense. The honest assessment was clear: there was no realistic path to retaining their home or sustaining any repayment plan. We advised bankruptcy as the only option that would write off all the debt and give them a genuine fresh start.

Bankruptcy can sound like the end of the world. It is not. It is a formal, legal process designed to give people in impossible situations exactly what Mr. and Mrs. V needed: a clear resolution and a way back to financial stability. We prepared the application, handled all correspondence with the Insolvency Service, and accompanied them to County Court and the Official Receiver interview. We explained what would happen and what to expect, so the process felt less frightening.

One year later they were discharged. All debt was written off. They kept their home because the equity had been consumed by the mortgage shortfall — there was nothing for the Trustee to realise. They now have a genuine fresh start and have stopped living month to month in crisis. The household has financial stability again.

Bankruptcy — What You Need to Know

A Creditor Arrangement is a repayment plan where you make payments to your creditors over time on agreed terms. In bankruptcy, the debt goes away — you do not repay it. You are declared bankrupt by the court, an Official Receiver is appointed, and after a period (usually one year), your debts are written off and you are discharged.

Bankruptcy is not an option you choose lightly, but when the debts are so large or complex that repayment is genuinely impossible, it can be the right answer.

That depends on how much equity you have in your home and who has a share of it. If your home has significant equity, the Official Receiver (the Trustee in Bankruptcy) will want to realise that value by selling it. It can be bought by a family member or friend on your behalf, but if it is not, then the Trustee may require you to sell it to realise the equity.

If you have little or no equity — or if a family member buys the equity from the Trustee — you can keep your home. We have helped many clients keep their homes despite going bankrupt, but each situation is different and it is complex.

Bankruptcy will appear on the public register until three months after you are discharged (roughly 15 months after the bankruptcy order). Some employers may ask about it. Your credit file will show the bankruptcy for six years from the date of the order. After that, it disappears from your credit file completely — even if you have not finished repaying any debts.

Some lenders will lend to people with a bankruptcy on their file, but others will not. After six years, your credit file is clean and you can borrow normally. We recommend talking to a mortgage broker if you want to know what might be possible in your situation.

An Official Receiver is a court official who works for the Insolvency Service, a government institution. They are appointed to manage your bankruptcy. They investigate your finances, interview you about how you got into debt, and manage your estate. They are unlikely to be the actual person who interviews you — usually an Examiner on their team will conduct the interview.

The Official Receiver's job is to understand your situation and, if appropriate, arrange for your debts to be written off at discharge.

Not directly — but if you are financially associated (for example, joint accounts, joint debts, or a joint mortgage), creditors may have access to your partner's credit file when making lending decisions. Your bankruptcy will appear on your credit file for six years. If your partner is on joint debts with you, those debts will be included in your bankruptcy and written off, but your partner may still be liable for their share unless they also go bankrupt or reach a separate arrangement.

Most bankruptcies are discharged after 12 months. Some may take longer depending on your conduct or circumstances. Discharge means you are no longer subject to bankruptcy restrictions. During bankruptcy, you cannot borrow more than £500 without disclosing you are an undischarged bankrupt, and you cannot be a company director. There are other restrictions, but these are the ones that impact most people.

At discharge, those restrictions end. The bankruptcy remains visible on the public register for 15 months after the order, and on your credit file for six years from the date of the order.
